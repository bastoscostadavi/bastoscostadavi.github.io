Davi Bastos Costa

I am a Schmidt AI in Science Postdoctoral Fellow at the University of Chicago, working with Chenhao Tan at the Chicago Human + AI Lab and with James Evans at Knowledge Lab. My research is on AI for science and on AI safety. The first I think of as computational philosophy of science. A physicist confronting an open problem — dark matter, neutrino masses, the hierarchy problem — proposes one extension of the Standard Model and works out by hand whether it helps; language models make it possible to survey that space at scale and check which extensions actually solve which problems. Hopefully one of them is correct.

Before Chicago I was a postdoctoral fellow at the TELUS Digital Research Hub at the University of São Paulo, working on AI safety with Renato Vicente. Three projects came out of it: Mini-Mafia, a social deduction game stripped down far enough that a closed-form expression predicts collective outcomes from three per-model capabilities; moral susceptibility and robustness, metrics for how much persona conditioning moves a model’s moral judgments and how stably it holds any one character; and persona-model collapse, behavioral evidence that emergent misalignment degrades a model’s capacity to simulate characters at all.

I came to this from theoretical physics. My PhD, at the University of Chicago with Clay Córdova, was on non-invertible symmetries in finite-group gauge theories: symmetry generalized beyond group theory, where fusing two operators returns a sum instead of an inverse. My master’s, at the University of São Paulo with Gustavo Burdman, was particle physics proper — twin Higgs models, dark matter, neutrino masses — with a stretch in 2019 at the Fermilab Theory Division with Roni Harnik. If the master’s was normal science, where an open problem motivates one more extension of the Standard Model, the PhD was an attempt at the other kind.

I arrived at physics sideways. I started university in geography, reading sociology and economics, and got caught by the question of what separates science from everything else — Popper, then Kuhn, then Mario Bunge. That question is what sent me to physics. As an undergraduate I worked with Nestor Caticha on the statistical mechanics of neural networks and emergent collective behavior in social systems, which is not far from what I do now. The trajectory is less of a departure than it looks: philosophy of science, done computationally.
